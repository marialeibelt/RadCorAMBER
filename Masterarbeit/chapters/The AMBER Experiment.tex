\chapter{The AMBER Experiment}\label{chap:AMBER}

\section{Overview of the AMBER Experiment}

The AMBER (Apparatus for Meson and Baryon Experimental Research) experiment, designated NA66, is a fixed-target experiment located in the EHN2 experimental hall in the North Area of CERN, as shown in \autoref{fig:LHC}. It uses the M2 beam line of the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS), which provides high-energy muon and hadron beams to the experiment.

AMBER builds on the infrastructure and large-acceptance spectrometer of the former COMPASS experiment and extends it with detector systems developed for its new physics program. Its measurements cover several topics in hadron physics, including hadron structure, spectroscopy, and a high-precision determination of the proton charge radius.
For the proton-radius measurement, AMBER studies muon--proton scattering at very small four-momentum transfers \(1\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{GeV}^2 < Q^2 < 4\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{GeV}^2\), where the electric form factor is particularly sensitive to the proton charge radius.

\section{Experimental Setup for the Proton Radius Measurement}
The proton-radius measurement is performed using a \(\SI{100}{GeV}\) muon beam. The beam is delivered to the detector in spills. A spill refers to a single period during which the beam is extracted from the accelerator and delivered to the experiment. For AMBER, a spill is approximately \(\qty{4.8}{\second}\) long. Therefore, an intensity of approximately \(4\times10^6\) muons per spill corresponds to an average rate of about \(8.3\times10^5\) muons per second that hit the target during the spill. 

The beam is directed onto an active hydrogen target operated at a pressure of \(\SI{20}{bar}\). The high gas pressure increases the target density and therefore the probability of muon--proton interactions. Since hydrogen provides a clean proton target, it allows the proton kinematics to be reconstructed without additional nuclear effects. 

The recoil proton is reconstructed inside the target using a Time Projection Chamber (TPC), while the incoming and scattered muons are measured by dedicated tracking detectors and the downstream spectrometer. An overview of the experimental setup is shown in \autoref{fig:setup}.

The direction of the incoming beam defines the longitudinal axis of the experiment. Detector components located before the interaction point with respect to the beam direction are referred to as upstream, whereas components located after the interaction point are called downstream.

The main detector components relevant for the proton-radius measurement are described in the following.

\paragraph{Unified Tracking Stations}
Four Unified Tracking Stations (UTS) surround the target region, with two stations located upstream and two downstream of the TPC. Their main purpose is to reconstruct the trajectories of the incoming and scattered muons and thereby determine the small muon scattering angles with high precision.

Each UTS combines Silicon Pixel Detectors (SPD) based on ALPIDE Monolithic Active Pixel Sensors (MAPS) with Scintillating Fiber Hodoscopes (SFH). The ALPIDE sensors provide the high spatial resolution required for precise track reconstruction. Their small pixel size of approximately \(28\times28\,\mu\mathrm{m}^2\) allows the position of the traversing muon to be measured accurately.

The SFH complement the pixel detectors by providing precise timing information. This is particularly important because the comparatively long integration time of the ALPIDE sensors can result in hits from several beam particles being recorded within the same time window. The time information provided by the SFH allows these ambiguities to be resolved and helps to associate the reconstructed muon tracks with the corresponding event in the TPC.

\paragraph{Time Projection Chamber}

The TPC serves as an active hydrogen target. It is operated with hydrogen gas at a pressure of \qty{20}{\bar} and is divided into two drift cells, each with a length of \qty{40}{\centi\meter}. In total, the system contains approximately \qty{20}{\meter\cubed} of hydrogen. Thin carbon-fiber composite windows are used where the muon beam enters and leaves the TPC in order to minimize interactions of the beam particles with detector material.

When a charged recoil proton traverses the hydrogen gas, it ionizes the gas molecules and creates electron--ion pairs along its trajectory. A high voltage of approximately \qty{100}{\kilo\volt} generates the electric drift field, causing the liberated electrons to drift towards the readout plane. A Frisch grid is placed in front of the anode to shield it from the drifting electrons until they reach the readout region. This reduces the dependence of the measured signal on the position at which the ionization was produced and improves the energy and time resolution.

The anode is segmented into pads, allowing the position and arrival time of the electrons to be measured and the three-dimensional proton track to be reconstructed. 

In addition to the track direction, the energy deposited by the proton provides information about its kinetic energy. For an initial proton at rest, the squared four-momentum transfer is directly related to the recoil-proton kinetic energy. The TPC therefore provides direct access to the recoil-proton kinematics and to the momentum transfer of the scattering process.

To maintain stable detector operation, the hydrogen is continuously recirculated and filtered, keeping impurity levels below approximately \(1\,\mathrm{ppm}\).

\paragraph{Spectrometer Magnets}

Downstream of the target region, the AMBER spectrometer contains two dipole magnets, SM1 and SM2. For the proton-radius measurement, the first magnet, SM1, is switched off, while the second dipole magnet, SM2, is operated. The deflection in SM2, together with downstream tracking detectors such as the GEM stations, allows the momentum of the scattered muon to be reconstructed.

Since elastic muon--proton scattering in the investigated low-\(Q^2\) region is strongly forward-peaked, precise reconstruction of the small scattering angles and the high-momentum outgoing muon is essential.

\paragraph{Electromagnetic and Hadronic Calorimeters}

The AMBER spectrometer features a two-stage calorimeter system containing two electromagnetic calorimeters (ECAL1 and ECAL2) and two hadronic calorimeters (HCAL1 and HCAL2). The calorimeters measure the energy of particles through the detection of the energy deposited in active detector material.

Electromagnetic calorimeters are primarily sensitive to electrons and photons, which produce electromagnetic showers in the detector material, whereas hadronic calorimeters are designed to measure strongly interacting particles.

For the proton-radius measurement, ECAL2 is particularly important because it provides sensitivity to photons emitted in radiative muon--proton scattering. Such photon emission modifies the kinematics of the observed final state and therefore has to be taken into account in the determination of radiative corrections. Although ECAL2 did not provide data for the proton-radius run before Long Shutdown 3, future measurements will be important for comparisons with theoretical predictions and the simulation framework developed in this thesis.

\paragraph{Muon Filters}

Muon identification is provided by the downstream AMBER spectrometer. Dedicated muon-filter systems consist of thick absorber material followed by tracking detectors. Hadrons and electromagnetic shower components are strongly attenuated in the absorber, whereas high-energy muons can penetrate the material. Tracks detected downstream of the absorber can therefore be matched to reconstructed spectrometer tracks to identify the scattered muon.
