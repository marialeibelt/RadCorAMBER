\label{chap:Theory}
\acresetall

Throughout this chapter, natural units with \(\hbar=c=1\) and the metric convention
\[
g^{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)
\]
are used.

\section{From Transition Rates to Scattering Cross Sections}

The transition rate of a quantum mechanical process is described by Fermi's golden rule. It relates the probability of a transition between an initial and a final state to the interaction matrix element and the available phase space,

\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{i\rightarrow f}
    =
    2\pi
    \left|\mathcal{M}_{fi}\right|^2
    \rho(E_f).
\end{equation}

Here, \(\mathcal{M}_{fi}\) is the transition matrix element and contains the dynamical information about the interaction. The factor \(\rho(E_f)\) describes the density of accessible final states and therefore contains the kinematic information of the process. It depends on the masses, energies, and momenta of the particles involved.

In relativistic quantum field theory, the corresponding quantity is the invariant scattering amplitude \(\mathcal{M}\), which is obtained from the relevant Feynman diagrams. The nonrelativistic transition matrix element \(\mathcal{M}_{fi}\) and the relativistic invariant amplitude \(\mathcal{M}\) play similar roles, but are defined using different normalization conventions for the particle states.

For the decay of one initial particle with four-momentum \(p_1\) into \(n\) final-state particles, the differential transition rate is given by

\begin{equation}
    d\Gamma
    =
    \frac{(2\pi)^4}{2E_1}
    |\mathcal{M}|^2
    \delta^{(4)}
    \left(
        p_1-\sum_f p_f
    \right)
    d\Phi_n ,
\end{equation}

where the Lorentz-invariant phase-space element is

\begin{equation}
    d\Phi_n
    =
    \prod_f
    \frac{d^3\mathbf{p}_f}{(2\pi)^3 2E_f}.
\end{equation}

The four-dimensional delta function ensures conservation of energy and momentum. For scattering processes, the relevant observable is not the transition rate itself but the cross section. It is obtained by normalizing the transition rate to the incident particle flux,

\begin{equation}
    d\sigma
    =
    \frac{d\Gamma}{\text{flux}}.
\end{equation}

The flux factor accounts for the relative motion of the incoming particles and normalizes the transition probability to the number of possible collisions. For a general \(2\rightarrow n\) scattering process, the Lorentz-invariant cross section is

\begin{equation}
    d\sigma
    =
    \frac{(2\pi)^4\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2}}
    {4\sqrt{(p_1\cdot p_2)^2-m_1^2m_2^2}}
    \delta^{(4)}
    \left(
        p_1+p_2-\sum_f p_f
    \right)
    d\Phi_n .
\end{equation}

For an unpolarized process, the bar denotes an average over the initial-state spins and a sum over the final-state spins.


\section{Non-Radiative Kinematics}

The starting point for the following discussion is the non-radiative elastic muon--proton scattering process

\begin{equation}
    p(p_1)+\mu(p_2)\rightarrow p(p_3)+\mu(p_4).
\end{equation}

In the laboratory frame, the initial proton is at rest. The incoming four-momenta are therefore

\begin{equation}
    p_1=(m_p,\mathbf{0}),
    \qquad
    p_2=(E_\mu,\mathbf{p}_\mu),
\end{equation}

where \(m_p\) is the proton mass and \(E_\mu\) is the energy of the incoming muon. The outgoing proton and muon are described by

\begin{equation}
    p_3=(E'_p,\mathbf{p}'_p),
    \qquad
    p_4=(E'_\mu,\mathbf{p}'_\mu).
\end{equation}

Energy and momentum conservation require

\begin{equation}
    p_1+p_2=p_3+p_4.
\end{equation}

At leading order, the interaction is mediated by the exchange of a virtual photon with four-momentum

\begin{equation}
    q=p_2-p_4=p_3-p_1 .
\end{equation}

The invariant momentum transfer is defined as

\begin{equation}
    Q^2=-q^2,
\end{equation}

such that \(Q^2>0\) for the spacelike momentum transfers relevant to elastic scattering. The value of \(Q^2\) determines the spatial resolution of the interaction: larger momentum transfers correspond to smaller distance scales being probed inside the proton.

The Mandelstam variables provide a convenient Lorentz-invariant description of the scattering kinematics. For the elastic \(2\rightarrow2\) process considered here, they are

\[
s=(p_1+p_2)^2
=(p_3+p_4)^2
=m_p^2+m_\mu^2+2m_pE_\mu,
\]

\begin{equation}
    t=(p_1-p_3)^2
    =(p_2-p_4)^2
    =2m_p^2-2m_pE'_p
    =-Q^2,
    \label{eq:t}
\end{equation}

and

\[
u=(p_1-p_4)^2
=(p_2-p_3)^2
=m_p^2+m_\mu^2-2m_pE'_\mu.
\]

They satisfy

\[
s+t+u=2m_p^2+2m_\mu^2.
\]


\section{Differential Cross Section for Elastic Muon--Proton Scattering}

The differential cross section for elastic muon--proton scattering can be expressed in terms of the Mandelstam variables and the invariant matrix element. For a general \(2\rightarrow2\) process,

\[
\frac{d\sigma}{dt}
=
\frac{1}{64\pi s\,p_{\mathrm{cm}}^2}
\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2},
\]

where \(p_{\mathrm{cm}}\) denotes the magnitude of either incoming particle momentum in the center-of-mass frame. It can be expressed using the Källén function,

\[
\lambda(s,m_\mu^2,m_p^2)
=
s^2+m_\mu^4+m_p^4
-2s(m_\mu^2+m_p^2)
-2m_\mu^2m_p^2,
\]

through

\[
p_{\mathrm{cm}}^2
=
\frac{\lambda(s,m_\mu^2,m_p^2)}{4s}.
\]

The differential cross section can therefore be written in the Lorentz-invariant form

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\sigma}{dt}
=
\frac{1}{16\pi\lambda(s,m_\mu^2,m_p^2)}
\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2}.
\label{eq:dsigma_dt}
\end{equation}

While \(d\sigma/dt\) provides a compact invariant description, the experimentally accessible quantities are the energy and scattering angle of the outgoing muon. It is therefore useful to express the cross section in terms of the solid angle,

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}
=
\frac{d\sigma}{dt}
\left|
\frac{dt}{d\Omega}
\right|.
\end{equation}

Since \(t=-Q^2\), see \autoref{eq:t}, the transformation factor becomes

\begin{equation}
\left|
\frac{dt}{d\Omega}
\right|
=
\frac{dQ^2}{d\Omega}.
\end{equation}

No massless approximation is made for the muon at this stage. In the laboratory frame, the magnitudes of the incoming and outgoing muon three-momenta are

\begin{equation}
p_\mu=\sqrt{E_\mu^2-m_\mu^2},
\qquad
p'_\mu=\sqrt{{E'_\mu}^2-m_\mu^2}.
\end{equation}

The outgoing proton is a real, on-shell particle and therefore satisfies \(p_3^2=m_p^2\). Since \(p_3=p_1+q\), this condition gives

\begin{equation}
Q^2=2m_p(E_\mu-E'_\mu).
\label{eq:Q2_energy_transfer}
\end{equation}

Independently, evaluating \(Q^2=-(p_2-p_4)^2\) gives

\begin{equation}
Q^2
=
2\left(
E_\mu E'_\mu
-p_\mu p'_\mu\cos\theta_\mu
-m_\mu^2
\right),
\label{eq:Q2_massive_angle}
\end{equation}

where \(\theta_\mu\) is the laboratory scattering angle of the outgoing muon. Equations~\eqref{eq:Q2_energy_transfer} and \eqref{eq:Q2_massive_angle} determine the final-state energy and momentum transfer for a given incoming energy and scattering angle. Solving for the physical outgoing-muon energy gives

\begin{equation}
E'_\mu
=
\frac{
(m_p+E_\mu)(m_pE_\mu+m_\mu^2)
+p_\mu^2\cos\theta_\mu
\sqrt{m_p^2-m_\mu^2\sin^2\theta_\mu}
}{
(m_p+E_\mu)^2-p_\mu^2\cos^2\theta_\mu
}.
\label{eq:outgoing_muon_energy}
\end{equation}

For an azimuthally symmetric unpolarized cross section, the azimuthal angle can be integrated from \(0\) to \(2\pi\), such that

\begin{equation}
d\Omega
=
2\pi\sin\theta_\mu\,d\theta_\mu.
\end{equation}

The corresponding transformation is therefore

\[
\frac{dQ^2}{d\Omega}
=
\frac{1}{2\pi\sin\theta_\mu}
\left|
\frac{dQ^2}{d\theta_\mu}
\right|.
\]

Differentiating the exact kinematic relations gives

\begin{equation}
\frac{dQ^2}{d\theta_\mu}
=
\frac{
2m_p p_\mu (p'_\mu)^2\sin\theta_\mu
}{
p'_\mu(m_p+E_\mu)
-p_\mu E'_\mu\cos\theta_\mu
}.
\end{equation}

Consequently,

\begin{equation}
\frac{dQ^2}{d\Omega}
=
\frac{
m_p p_\mu (p'_\mu)^2
}{
\pi\left[
p'_\mu(m_p+E_\mu)
-p_\mu E'_\mu\cos\theta_\mu
\right]
}.
\label{eq:massive_jacobian}
\end{equation}

In the laboratory frame, the Källén function satisfies

\[
\lambda(s,m_\mu^2,m_p^2)
=
4m_p^2p_\mu^2.
\]

Inserting this relation together with \autoref{eq:massive_jacobian} into \autoref{eq:dsigma_dt} gives

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}
=
\frac{(p'_\mu)^2}
{64\pi^2m_p p_\mu
\left[
p'_\mu(m_p+E_\mu)
-p_\mu E'_\mu\cos\theta_\mu
\right]}
\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2}.
\label{eq:diff_cross_section_exact}
\end{equation}

This expression contains the exact two-body phase-space and recoil factors for a finite muon mass. The remaining task is to determine the matrix element \(\mathcal{M}\) describing the electromagnetic interaction.


\section{Born Approximation}

The starting point for calculating the scattering amplitude is the interaction part of the QED Lagrangian. At leading order in quantum electrodynamics, referred to as the Born approximation, muon--proton scattering is described by the exchange of a single virtual photon.

For a charged fermion, the electromagnetic interaction is described by

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}
=
-eQ_f\overline{\psi}\gamma^\mu\psi A_\mu,
\]

where \(\psi\) is the Dirac spinor field representing the charged fermion, \(A_\mu\) is the electromagnetic four-potential, \(\gamma^\mu\) are the Dirac gamma matrices, \(e>0\) is the elementary charge, and \(Q_f\) is the fermion charge in units of \(e\).

Introducing the electromagnetic current

\[
j^\mu_{\mathrm{em}}
=
Q_f\overline{\psi}\gamma^\mu\psi,
\]

the interaction Lagrangian becomes

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}
=
-ej^\mu_{\mathrm{em}}A_\mu.
\]

This expression directly shows that the electromagnetic interaction arises from the coupling between the electromagnetic current and the electromagnetic field. In a scattering process, this interaction is represented diagrammatically by a vertex connecting a charged fermion to a photon.

For a point-like fermion such as the muon, the electromagnetic vertex follows directly from the QED interaction Lagrangian. Apart from the charge factor \(Q_\mu\), the corresponding Feynman rule is

\[
-ie\gamma^\mu.
\]

Including the incoming and outgoing muon spinors, the muon vertex is

\[
Q_\mu
\overline{u}(p_4)
(-ie\gamma^\mu)
u(p_2),
\]

where \(u(p_2)\) and \(\overline{u}(p_4)\) describe the incoming and outgoing muon, respectively.

For a composite particle such as the proton, the electromagnetic vertex is modified by its internal structure. This modification is described by electromagnetic form factors, which characterize the proton's internal charge and magnetization structure as a function of the momentum transfer. The proton vertex can be written as

\[
\overline{u}(p_3)
(-ie\Gamma^\nu(Q^2))
u(p_1),
\]

with the vertex function

\begin{equation}
\Gamma^\nu(Q^2)
=
F_1(Q^2)\gamma^\nu
+
\frac{i\sigma^{\nu\rho}q_\rho}{2m_p}
F_2(Q^2),
\label{eq:proton_vertex}
\end{equation}

where

\[
\sigma^{\nu\rho}
=
\frac{i}{2}
[\gamma^\nu,\gamma^\rho].
\]

The Dirac form factor \(F_1\) describes the charge-like part of the proton's electromagnetic interaction, while the Pauli form factor \(F_2\) accounts for its anomalous magnetic contribution. At zero momentum transfer, they satisfy

\[
F_1(0)=1,
\qquad
F_2(0)=\kappa_p=\mu_p-1,
\]

where \(\kappa_p\) is the anomalous magnetic moment and
\(\mu_p=2.792847\) is the dimensionless proton magnetic moment in units of the nuclear magneton.

The \(Q^2\) dependence of the two proton form factors is illustrated in \autoref{fig:FormFac}. Both form factors change as the momentum transfer increases, reflecting the fact that the proton is not point-like. The comparison of the two calculation schemes also illustrates how different theoretical treatments of the hadronic structure affect the detailed \(Q^2\) dependence.

For the interpretation of elastic scattering, it is useful to introduce the Sachs form factors. They are related to the Dirac and Pauli form factors by

\begin{equation}
G_E(Q^2)
=
F_1(Q^2)
-
\frac{Q^2}{4m_p^2}F_2(Q^2)
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
G_M(Q^2)
=
F_1(Q^2)+F_2(Q^2).
\end{equation}

Their normalizations at zero momentum transfer are

\[
G_E(0)=1,
\qquad
G_M(0)=\mu_p.
\]

The Sachs form factors provide a more direct description of the electric and magnetic structure of the proton. Their \(Q^2\) dependence is shown in \autoref{fig:Sachs}. Both \(G_E\) and \(G_M\) decrease as the momentum transfer increases, indicating that the electromagnetic structure of the proton becomes resolved. Of particular importance for the proton-radius measurement is the behavior of \(G_E\) close to \(Q^2=0\). As discussed in the introduction, the slope of \(G_E\) in this region determines the proton charge radius. Measurements at very small \(Q^2\), such as those performed by AMBER, are therefore especially sensitive to this quantity.

The exchanged virtual photon connects the muon and proton vertices. Since it is an internal particle, it is represented by a propagator rather than an external photon wave function. In Feynman gauge, a convenient gauge choice in which the photon propagator takes a particularly simple form, it is given by

\[
\frac{-ig_{\mu\nu}}{q^2}.
\]

The muon and proton vertices carry the Lorentz indices \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), respectively, corresponding to the leptonic and hadronic spinor chains. The virtual-photon propagator connects these two currents, with the metric tensor \(g_{\mu\nu}\) contracting the Lorentz indices according to the Einstein summation convention.

Written with both Lorentz indices explicitly, the Born matrix element is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{Born}}
=
\left[
Q_\mu
\overline{u}(p_4)
(-ie\gamma^\mu)
u(p_2)
\right]
\frac{-ig_{\mu\nu}}{q^2}
\left[
\overline{u}(p_3)
(-ie\Gamma^\nu(Q^2))
u(p_1)
\right].
\end{equation}

The contraction with the metric tensor lowers the Lorentz index of the proton vertex,

\[
g_{\mu\nu}\Gamma^\nu(Q^2)
=
\Gamma_\mu(Q^2),
\]

so that the matrix element can be written more compactly as

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{Born}}
=
Q_\mu
\frac{e^2}{q^2}
\,
\overline{u}(p_4)\gamma^\mu u(p_2)
\,
\overline{u}(p_3)\Gamma_\mu(Q^2)u(p_1).
\label{eq:born_amplitude}
\end{equation}

The matrix element itself is not directly observable. The differential cross section depends on its squared modulus, summed over the unobserved final-state spins and averaged over the initial-state spins. For unpolarized muon--proton scattering,

\begin{equation}
\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2}
=
\frac{1}{4}
\sum_{s_1,s_2,s_3,s_4}
|\mathcal{M}|^2,
\end{equation}

where the factor \(1/4\) averages over the two possible spin states of each incoming spin-\(1/2\) particle.

Writing the spin labels explicitly,

\[
\mathcal{M}
=
Q_\mu\frac{e^2}{q^2}
\left[
\overline{u}(p_4,s_4)
\gamma^\mu
u(p_2,s_2)
\right]
\left[
\overline{u}(p_3,s_3)
\Gamma_\mu(Q^2)
u(p_1,s_1)
\right].
\]

Its complex conjugate is

\[
\mathcal{M}^*
=
Q_\mu\frac{e^2}{q^2}
\left[
\overline{u}(p_2,s_2)
\gamma^\nu
u(p_4,s_4)
\right]
\left[
\overline{u}(p_1,s_1)
\overline{\Gamma}_\nu(Q^2)
u(p_3,s_3)
\right],
\]

where

\[
\overline{\Gamma}_\nu
=
\gamma^0
\Gamma_\nu^\dagger
\gamma^0.
\]

Since \(Q_\mu=-1\), the charge factor satisfies \(Q_\mu^2=1\) in the squared amplitude. Thus,

\[
|\mathcal{M}|^2
=
\frac{e^4}{(q^2)^2}
\left[
\overline{u}(p_4)\gamma^\mu u(p_2)
\right]
\left[
\overline{u}(p_2)\gamma^\nu u(p_4)
\right]
\left[
\overline{u}(p_3)\Gamma_\mu u(p_1)
\right]
\left[
\overline{u}(p_1)\overline{\Gamma}_\nu u(p_3)
\right].
\]

To evaluate the spin sums, the completeness relation for Dirac spinors is used,

\[
\sum_s
u(p,s)\overline{u}(p,s)
=
\slashed{p}+m,
\]

where

\[
\slashed{p}
=
\gamma^\mu p_\mu.
\]

This relation converts the products of spinors into traces over gamma matrices. The spin-averaged squared matrix element can consequently be written as

\begin{equation}
\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2}
=
\frac{e^4}{4(q^2)^2}
L^{\mu\nu}H_{\mu\nu},
\label{eq:tensor_factorization}
\end{equation}

where the leptonic tensor is

\[
L^{\mu\nu}
=
\operatorname{Tr}
\left[
(\slashed{p}_4+m_\mu)
\gamma^\mu
(\slashed{p}_2+m_\mu)
\gamma^\nu
\right],
\]

and the hadronic tensor is

\[
H_{\mu\nu}
=
\operatorname{Tr}
\left[
(\slashed{p}_3+m_p)
\Gamma_\mu(Q^2)
(\slashed{p}_1+m_p)
\overline{\Gamma}_\nu(Q^2)
\right].
\]

The factorization into a leptonic and a hadronic tensor separates the two parts of the interaction. The leptonic tensor describes the coupling of the virtual photon to the point-like muon, whereas the hadronic tensor contains the electromagnetic structure of the proton through its form factors.

For the muon, the leptonic tensor can be evaluated using the standard gamma-matrix trace identities,

\begin{equation}
L^{\mu\nu}
=
4\left[
p_4^\mu p_2^\nu
+p_4^\nu p_2^\mu
-g^{\mu\nu}
\left(
p_2\cdot p_4-m_\mu^2
\right)
\right].
\label{eq:massive_leptonic_tensor}
\end{equation}

The leptonic tensor satisfies current conservation and is therefore transverse to the transferred four-momentum,

\[
q_\mu L^{\mu\nu}
=
q_\nu L^{\mu\nu}
=
0.
\]

To evaluate the hadronic tensor, it is convenient to introduce

\begin{equation}
\tau
=
\frac{Q^2}{4m_p^2}
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}

and

\[
P^\mu
=
p_1^\mu+p_3^\mu.
\]

The on-shell conditions imply

\[
P\cdot q=0,
\qquad
P^2=4m_p^2+Q^2.
\]

Expressed in terms of the Sachs form factors, the hadronic tensor takes the form

\begin{equation}
H_{\mu\nu}
=
2\frac{G_E^2(Q^2)+\tau G_M^2(Q^2)}
{1+\tau}
P_\mu P_\nu
-
2G_M^2(Q^2)
\left(
q_\mu q_\nu
+
Q^2g_{\mu\nu}
\right).
\label{eq:hadronic_tensor_sachs}
\end{equation}

Because of current conservation, terms proportional to \(q_\mu q_\nu\) do not contribute when the hadronic tensor is contracted with the leptonic tensor. Defining

\begin{equation}
A(Q^2)
=
\frac{G_E^2(Q^2)+\tau G_M^2(Q^2)}
{1+\tau},
\end{equation}

the contraction of \autoref{eq:massive_leptonic_tensor} and \autoref{eq:hadronic_tensor_sachs} gives the exact spin-averaged Born matrix element,

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\overline{|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{Born}}|^2}
=
\frac{e^4}{Q^4}
\Bigl\{&
A(Q^2)
\left[
(s-u)^2
-
Q^2\left(4m_p^2+Q^2\right)
\right]
\\
&+
2G_M^2(Q^2)Q^2
\left(Q^2-2m_\mu^2\right)
\Bigr\}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:born_matrix_element_massive}
\end{equation}

Inserting \autoref{eq:born_matrix_element_massive} into \autoref{eq:diff_cross_section_exact} yields the exact laboratory-frame Born cross section,

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\frac{d\sigma_{\mathrm{Born}}}{d\Omega}
={}&
\frac{1}{64\pi^2m_p p_\mu}
\frac{(p'_\mu)^2}
{
p'_\mu(m_p+E_\mu)
-
p_\mu E'_\mu\cos\theta_\mu
}
\frac{e^4}{Q^4}
\\
&\times
\left\{
A(Q^2)
\left[
(s-u)^2
-
Q^2\left(4m_p^2+Q^2\right)
\right]
+
2G_M^2(Q^2)Q^2
\left(Q^2-2m_\mu^2\right)
\right\}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:born_cross_section_exact}
\end{equation}


\subsection{Ultrarelativistic Limit}

For the \SI{100}{GeV} muon beam used in the AMBER proton-radius measurement, the incoming muon energy is much larger than the muon mass. It is therefore useful to consider the ultrarelativistic limit

\[
E_\mu\gg m_\mu.
\]

In this limit,

\[
p_\mu\simeq E_\mu,
\qquad
p'_\mu\simeq E'_\mu,
\]

and the outgoing-muon energy becomes

\begin{equation}
E'_\mu
=
\frac{E_\mu}
{
1+\dfrac{2E_\mu}{m_p}
\sin^2\left(\dfrac{\theta_\mu}{2}\right)
}.
\label{eq:Eprime_ultrarel}
\end{equation}

The momentum transfer can then be written as

\[
Q^2
=
4E_\mu E'_\mu
\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta_\mu}{2}\right),
\]

and differentiation with respect to the scattering angle gives

\begin{equation}
\frac{dQ^2}{d\theta_\mu}
=
\frac{
2E_\mu^2\sin\theta_\mu
}{
\left[
1+\dfrac{2E_\mu}{m_p}
\sin^2\left(\dfrac{\theta_\mu}{2}\right)
\right]^2
}.
\label{eq:dQ2_dtheta_ultrarel}
\end{equation}

The exact phase-space expression in \autoref{eq:diff_cross_section_exact} correspondingly simplifies to

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}
=
\frac{
\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2}
}{
64\pi^2m_p^2
\left[
1+\dfrac{2E_\mu}{m_p}
\sin^2\left(\dfrac{\theta_\mu}{2}\right)
\right]^2
}.
\label{eq:diff_cross_section_ultrarel}
\end{equation}

The muon-mass terms also disappear from the leptonic tensor,

\[
L^{\mu\nu}
=
4
\left(
p_4^\mu p_2^\nu
+
p_4^\nu p_2^\mu
-
g^{\mu\nu}p_4\cdot p_2
\right).
\]

The hadronic tensor in \autoref{eq:hadronic_tensor_sachs}, however, remains unchanged, since the ultrarelativistic approximation concerns the muon kinematics rather than the proton structure.

Combining the leptonic and hadronic tensors leads to the Rosenbluth form of the elastic Born cross section,

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\sigma_{\mathrm{Born}}}{d\Omega}
=
\frac{d\sigma_{\mathrm{Mott}}}{d\Omega}
\,
\frac{
\tau G_M^2(Q^2)
+
\epsilon G_E^2(Q^2)
}{
\epsilon(1+\tau)
}.
\label{eq:rosenbluth}
\end{equation}

Here, the Mott factor describes scattering from a point-like charged target. With the recoil factor included in the convention used here, it is

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\sigma_{\mathrm{Mott}}}{d\Omega}
=
\frac{
\alpha^2
\cos^2\left(\theta_\mu/2\right)
}{
4E_\mu^2
\sin^4\left(\theta_\mu/2\right)
}
\frac{E'_\mu}{E_\mu},
\label{eq:mott_cross_section}
\end{equation}

where

\[
\alpha=\frac{e^2}{4\pi}
\]

is the fine-structure constant. The virtual-photon polarization parameter is

\begin{equation}
\epsilon
=
\left[
1
+
2(1+\tau)
\tan^2\left(\frac{\theta_\mu}{2}\right)
\right]^{-1}.
\label{eq:epsilon}
\end{equation}

The Rosenbluth expression makes the role of the proton structure explicit: the electric and magnetic contributions enter separately through \(G_E\) and \(G_M\), while a point-like proton would correspond to structure-independent form factors.


For the numerical description used later in this thesis, the Sachs form factors are represented by the empirical dipole parametrization

\begin{equation}
G_D(Q^2)
=
\left(
1+\frac{Q^2}{\Lambda^2}
\right)^{-2},
\label{eq:dipole_form_factor}
\end{equation}

with

\[
\Lambda^2
=
0.71\,\mathrm{GeV}^2.
\]

The proton electric and magnetic form factors are then parametrized as

\begin{equation}
G_E(Q^2)
=
G_D(Q^2)
=
\left(
1+\frac{Q^2}{0.71\,\mathrm{GeV}^2}
\right)^{-2},
\label{eq:GE_dipole}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
G_M(Q^2)
=
\mu_p G_D(Q^2)
=
\mu_p
\left(
1+\frac{Q^2}{0.71\,\mathrm{GeV}^2}
\right)^{-2}.
\label{eq:GM_dipole}
\end{equation}


\section{Radiative Corrections}
The Born approximation provides the leading contribution to elastic muon--proton scattering, but a precision determination of the proton charge radius requires higher-order QED effects to be included. In this thesis, radiative corrections are considered through next-to-leading order (NLO).

The Born amplitude is of order \(e^2\), corresponding to a cross section of order \(\alpha^2\). At NLO, the cross section receives corrections of order \(\alpha^3\) from two different types of contributions: virtual one-loop corrections and real-photon emission. \autoref{fig:processes} illustrates these processes. 

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.\textwidth]{figures/Processes_Mehran.pdf}
    \caption{Leading-order and next-to-leading-order contributions to muon--proton scattering. At leading order, the interaction proceeds through the exchange of a single virtual photon. At NLO, virtual loop corrections and real-photon emission contribute. Figure taken from \cite{M.Depour}.}
    \label{fig:processes}
\end{figure}

The complete NLO correczred spin-averaged amplitude is given by
\(
\overline{\mathcal{M}^2}=\mathcal{\chi}_{Born}+\mathcal{\chi}_{virtual}+\mathcal{\chi}_{Bremsstrahlung},
\)
where the LO Born contribution is just \(\mathcal{\chi}_{Born}=\mathcal{M}_{Born}\) (\autoref{eq:born_matrix_element_massive}) and the NLO correction contributions will be detailed in the following two subsections.

\subsection{One-Loop Virtual Processes}
Virtual corrections arise from one-loop diagrams. They include self-energy, vertex, vacuum-polarization, and box contributions such as two-photon exchange. 

Since the virtual diagrams lead to the same external final state as the Born process, their contribution to the NLO cross section enters through the interference between the Born and one-loop amplitudes,

\[
2\,\mathrm{Re}
\left(
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{Born}}^*
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{virtual}}
\right).
\]

Ultraviolet divergences appearing in the loop integrals are removed through renormalization. Infrared divergences, however, cancel only when the virtual contribution is combined consistently with the corresponding real-emission contribution for an infrared-safe observable.

\paragraph{Real Photon Emission}

The real NLO contribution is the bremsstrahlung process

\begin{equation}
p\mu
\rightarrow
p\mu\gamma,
\label{eq:real_emission}
\end{equation}

in which an additional photon is emitted from one of the charged external particles. In contrast to the non-radiative \(2\rightarrow2\) process, real photon emission produces a three-body final state. The photon carries energy and momentum away from the muon--proton system and can therefore significantly alter observables such as the outgoing-muon energy, scattering angle, and reconstructed momentum transfer.

The virtual and real contributions must be combined using the same definition of the measured observable. Their sum is infrared finite, while the resulting numerical correction depends on the experimental phase-space definition, acceptance requirements, and photon-energy cuts. This makes a fully differential treatment of radiative corrections particularly important for the AMBER proton-radius measurement.
