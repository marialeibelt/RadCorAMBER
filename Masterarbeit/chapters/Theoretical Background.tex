\chapter{Theoretical Background on Elastic Muon-Proton Scattering}\label{chap:Theory}
Throughout this chapter, natural units with \(\hbar=c=1\) and the metric convention \(g^{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)\) are used.

\section{From Transition Rates to Scattering Cross Sections}

The transition rate of a quantum mechanical process is described by Fermi's golden rule. It relates the probability of a transition between an initial state and a final state to the interaction matrix element and the available phase space:

\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{i\rightarrow f}
    =
    2\pi
    \left|\mathcal{M}_{fi}\right|^2
    \rho(E_f),
\end{equation}

where \(\mathcal{M}_{fi}\) is the matrix element of the interaction and contains all information about the dynamics of the process. It is calculated from the corresponding Feynman diagrams. The factor \(\rho(E_f)\) describes the density of accessible final states and contains only kinematic information. It depends on the masses, energies, and momenta of the particles involved. A larger available phase space increases the number of possible final states and therefore enhances the transition probability. The nonrelativistic matrix element \(\mathcal{M}_{fi}\) and the invariant amplitude \(\mathcal{M}\) used below play analogous roles, although their state-normalization conventions are different.

For the decay of one initial particle with four-momentum \(p_1\) into \(n\) final-state particles, the differential transition rate is given by

\begin{equation}
    d\Gamma
    =
    \frac{(2\pi)^4}{2E_1}
    |\mathcal{M}|^2
    \delta^{(4)}
    \left(
        p_1-\sum_f p_f
    \right)
    d\Phi_n ,
\end{equation}

where the Lorentz-invariant phase space element is defined as

\begin{equation}
    d\Phi_n
    =
    \prod_f
    \frac{d^3\mathbf{p}_f}{(2\pi)^3 2E_f}.
\end{equation}

The four-dimensional delta function ensures conservation of energy and momentum. In scattering processes, the relevant observable is not the transition rate but the cross section. It is obtained by normalizing the transition rate to the incident particle flux,

\begin{equation}
    d\sigma
    =
    \frac{d\Gamma}{\text{flux}}.
\end{equation}
The flux factor accounts for the relative motion of the incoming particles and normalizes the transition rate to the number of possible collisions. For a general \(2\rightarrow n\) scattering process, this results in the Lorentz-invariant expression

\begin{equation}
    d\sigma
    =
    \frac{(2\pi)^4\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2}}
    {4\sqrt{(p_1\cdot p_2)^2-m_1^2m_2^2}}
    \delta^{(4)}
    \left(
        p_1+p_2-\sum_f p_f
    \right)
    d\Phi_n .
\end{equation}

For an unpolarized process, the bar denotes an average over the initial-state spins and a sum over the final-state spins.

\section{Non-Radiative Kinematics}
In this thesis, only elastic muon-proton scattering is considered,

\begin{equation}
    p(p_1)+\mu(p_2)\rightarrow p(p_3)+\mu(p_4).
\end{equation}

The proton is initially at rest, therefore the incoming four-momenta are given by

\begin{equation}
    p_1=(m_p,\mathbf{0}),
    \qquad
    p_2=(E_\mu,\mathbf{p}_\mu),
\end{equation}

where \(m_p\) is the proton mass and \(E_\mu\) is the energy of the incoming muon. The outgoing proton and muon are described by

\begin{equation}
    p_3=(E'_p,\mathbf{p}'_p),
    \qquad
    p_4=(E'_\mu,\mathbf{p}'_\mu).
\end{equation}

Energy and momentum conservation require

\begin{equation}
    p_1+p_2=p_3+p_4.
\end{equation}

The interaction is mediated by the exchange of a virtual photon with four-momentum

\begin{equation}
    q=p_2-p_4=p_3-p_1 .
\end{equation}

The invariant momentum transfer is defined as

\begin{equation}
    Q^2=-q^2,
\end{equation}

where the positive sign convention for \(Q^2\) is commonly used in scattering experiments. The value of \(Q^2\) determines the resolution scale of the interaction: larger values of \(Q^2\) correspond to a smaller distance scale probed inside the proton.

The Mandelstam variables provide a convenient description of relativistic scattering processes. Because these variables are Lorentz invariant, they give a compact, frame-independent representation of scattering kinematics and cross sections. For a \(2\rightarrow2\) process, they are
\[
s=(p_1+p_2)^2=(p_3+p_4)^2=m_p^2+2m_pE_{\mu}+m_{\mu}^2,
\]
\begin{equation}
    t=(p_1-p_3)^2=(p_2-p_4)^2=2m_p^2-2m_pE'_p=-Q^2,
\label{eq:t}
\end{equation}

\[
u=(p_1-p_4)^2=(p_2-p_3)^2=m_p^2-2m_pE'_{\mu}+m_{\mu}^2.
\]
They satisfy
\[
s+t+u=2m_p^2+2m_\mu^2.
\]

\section{Differential Cross Section for Elastic Muon--Proton Scattering}

The differential cross section for elastic muon-proton scattering can be expressed in terms of the Mandelstam variables and the matrix element of the interaction. The general expression for a two-particle scattering process is given by

\[
\frac{d\sigma}{dt}
=
\frac{1}{64\pi s}
\frac{1}{|\mathbf{p}_{1,\mathrm{cm}}|^2}
\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2},
\]

where $|\mathbf{p}_{1,\mathrm{cm}}|$ is the magnitude of the incoming particle momentum in the center-of-mass frame. To express the momentum in terms of the Mandelstam variable $s$, the Källén function is introduced:

\[
\lambda(s,m_\mu^2,m_p^2)
=
s^2+m_\mu^4+m_p^4
-2s(m_\mu^2+m_p^2)
-2m_\mu^2m_p^2 .
\]

The center-of-mass momentum is related to the Källén function by

\[
|\mathbf{p}_{1,\mathrm{cm}}|^2
=
\frac{\lambda(s,m_\mu^2,m_p^2)}{4s}.
\]

Therefore, the differential cross section can be rewritten as

\[
\frac{d\sigma}{dt}
=
\frac{1}{16\pi\lambda(s,m_\mu^2,m_p^2)}
\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2} .
\]

This expression is written in a Lorentz-invariant form and can therefore be
evaluated in any reference frame.

However, the experimentally measured quantity is not directly $d\sigma/dt$. The detector measures the kinematic properties of the outgoing muon, in particular its scattering angle and energy. Therefore, the differential cross section is usually expressed as a function of the scattering angle:

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}
=
\frac{d\sigma}{dt}
\left|
\frac{dt}{d\Omega}
\right|.
\end{equation}

Since $t=-Q^2$ by definition, see \autoref{eq:t}, the absolute value of the
transformation factor is

\begin{equation}
\left|\frac{dt}{d\Omega}\right|
=
\frac{dQ^2}{d\Omega}.
\end{equation}

No massless approximation is made for the muon in the following derivation. In the
laboratory frame, the magnitudes of the incoming and outgoing muon momenta are

\begin{equation}
p_\mu=\sqrt{E_\mu^2-m_\mu^2},
\qquad
p'_\mu=\sqrt{{E'_\mu}^2-m_\mu^2}.
\end{equation}

The on-shell condition for the final-state proton,
$(p_1+q)^2=m_p^2$, gives

\begin{equation}
Q^2=2m_p(E_\mu-E'_\mu).
\label{eq:Q2_energy_transfer}
\end{equation}

Independently, evaluating $Q^2=-(p_2-p_4)^2$ yields

\begin{equation}
Q^2
=
2\left(
E_\mu E'_\mu
-p_\mu p'_\mu\cos\theta_\mu
-m_\mu^2
\right),
\label{eq:Q2_massive_angle}
\end{equation}

where $\theta_\mu$ is the laboratory scattering angle of the outgoing muon.
Equations~\eqref{eq:Q2_energy_transfer} and
\eqref{eq:Q2_massive_angle} determine $E'_\mu$ and $Q^2$ for a given beam
energy and scattering angle. Solving them for the physical outgoing-muon
energy gives

\begin{equation}
E'_\mu
=
\frac{
(m_p+E_\mu)(m_pE_\mu+m_\mu^2)
+p_\mu^2\cos\theta_\mu
\sqrt{m_p^2-m_\mu^2\sin^2\theta_\mu}
}{
(m_p+E_\mu)^2-p_\mu^2\cos^2\theta_\mu
}.
\label{eq:outgoing_muon_energy}
\end{equation}

For an azimuthally symmetric unpolarized cross section, the azimuthal angle has already been integrated from $0$ to $2\pi$, so that

\begin{equation}
d\Omega=2\pi\sin\theta_\mu\,d\theta_\mu.
\end{equation}


Using this, the transformation between the two differential cross sections becomes

\[
\frac{dQ^2}{d\Omega}
=
\frac{1}{2\pi\sin\theta_\mu}
\left|
\frac{dQ^2}{d\theta_\mu}
\right|.
\]

Differentiating the exact kinematic relations gives

\begin{equation}
\frac{dQ^2}{d\theta_\mu}
=
\frac{
2m_p p_\mu {p'_\mu}^2\sin\theta_\mu
}{
p'_\mu(m_p+E_\mu)-p_\mu E'_\mu\cos\theta_\mu
}.
\end{equation}

Consequently, the exact transformation factor is

\begin{equation}
\frac{dQ^2}{d\Omega}
=
\frac{
m_p p_\mu {p'_\mu}^2
}{
\pi\left[
p'_\mu(m_p+E_\mu)-p_\mu E'_\mu\cos\theta_\mu
\right]
}.
\label{eq:massive_jacobian}
\end{equation}

In the laboratory frame, the Käll\'en function satisfies $\lambda(s,m_\mu^2,m_p^2)=4m_p^2p_\mu^2$. Inserting this relation and \autoref{eq:massive_jacobian} into the invariant cross section gives

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}
= \frac{{p'_\mu}^2}
{64\pi^2m_p p_\mu[p'_\mu(m_p+E_\mu)-p_\mu E'_\mu\cos\theta_\mu]}
\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2}.
\label{eq:diff_cross_section}
\end{equation}

This expression contains the exact two-body phase-space and recoil factors
for a finite muon mass. The remaining task is to determine the matrix element
$\mathcal{M}$ describing the electromagnetic interaction.

In the ultra-relativistic limit \(E_\mu \gg m_\mu\). It follows that \(p_{\mu}\approx E_\mu\) and the outgoing muon momentum can be approximated by \(p'_\mu\approx E'_\mu\). In this limit 

\begin{equation}
\frac{dQ^2}{d\theta_\mu}
=
\frac{
2E_\mu^2\sin\theta_\mu
}{
\left[
1+(2E_\mu/m_p)
\sin^2\left(\theta_\mu/2\right)
\right]^2
}.
\end{equation}

and the differential cross section simplifies to

\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}
    =
    \frac{1}{64\pi^2 m_p^2
[1+(2E_\mu/m_p)\sin^2(\theta_\mu/2)]^2}\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2}.
    \label{eq:diff_cross_section}
\end{equation}


\section{Born Approximation}
The starting point for calculating the scattering amplitude is the interaction part of the QED Lagrangian. At leading order in quantum electrodynamics (Born approximation), the interaction is described by the exchange of a single virtual photon.
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}} = -eQ_f\overline{\psi}\gamma^\mu\psi A_\mu,
\]
where $\psi$ is the Dirac spinor field representing the charged fermion, $A_\mu$ is the electromagnetic four-potential, $\gamma^\mu$ are the Dirac gamma matrices, $e>0$ is the elementary charge, and $Q_f$ is the fermion charge in units of $e$.
The interaction Lagrangian can therefore be written in terms of the electromagnetic current $j^\mu_{\mathrm{em}} = Q_f\overline{\psi}\gamma^\mu\psi$ as
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}} = -e j^\mu_{\mathrm{em}} A_\mu.
\]
This expression directly shows that the electromagnetic interaction arises from the coupling between the electromagnetic current and the electromagnetic field. In a scattering process, this coupling is represented diagrammatically by a vertex. The vertex therefore describes the interaction between a charged fermion and a photon. For a point-like fermion such as the muon, the electromagnetic vertex follows directly from the QED interaction Lagrangian. Apart from the charge factor $Q_\mu$, the corresponding Feynman rule is
\[
-ie\gamma^\mu,
\]
and, written in terms of the incoming and outgoing muon spinors, the vertex factor is
\[
Q_\mu\overline{u}(p_4)(-ie\gamma^\mu)u(p_2),
\]
where $u(p_2)$ and $\overline{u}(p_4)$ are the Dirac spinors for the incoming and outgoing muon, respectively.

For a composite particle like the proton, the electromagnetic vertex is modified by the internal structure of the particle. This modification is described by the electromagnetic form factors, which encode information about the spatial distribution of charge and magnetization within the proton. The vertex factor for the proton can be expressed as
\[
\overline{u}(p_3)(-ie\Gamma^\nu(Q^2))u(p_1),
\]
where $u(p_1)$ and $\overline{u}(p_3)$ are the Dirac spinors for the incoming and outgoing proton. In the spacelike region, the form factors are written as functions of the positive variable $Q^2=-q^2$; that is, $F_i(Q^2)\equiv F_i(q^2=-Q^2)$. The electromagnetic vertex function is
\[
\Gamma^\nu(Q^2) = F_1(Q^2)\gamma^\nu + \frac{i\sigma^{\nu\rho}q_\rho}{2m_p}F_2(Q^2),
\]
where $\sigma^{\nu\rho} = \frac{i}{2}[\gamma^\nu,\gamma^\rho]$ is the antisymmetric tensor constructed from the gamma matrices, and $m_p$ is the mass of the proton.

At zero momentum transfer, the Dirac and Pauli form factors are normalized as
\[
F_1(0)=1,
\qquad
F_2(0)=\mu_p-1,
\]
where $\mu_p$ is the nuclear magneton. The two form factors together describe the response of the proton's charge and magnetization structure to the electromagnetic probe. A more direct interpretation in terms of electric and magnetic distributions is provided by the Sachs form factors.

The Sachs form factors are related to the Dirac and Pauli form factors by
\[
G_E(Q^2) = F_1(Q^2) - \frac{Q^2}{4m_p^2}F_2(Q^2)
\]
and
\[
G_M(Q^2) = F_1(Q^2) + F_2(Q^2).
\]

The exchanged photon connects the muon and proton vertices. Since this photon is virtual, it is described by a photon propagator rather than by an external photon wave function. In Feynman gauge, the propagator is
\[
\frac{-ig_{\mu\nu}}{q^2},
\]
where $g_{\mu\nu}$ is the metric tensor. The muon and proton vertices carry the Lorentz indices $\mu$ and $\nu$, respectively. These indices are contracted through the virtual-photon propagator, whose metric tensor $g_{\mu\nu}$ connects the leptonic and hadronic currents according to the Einstein summation convention.

Written with both Lorentz indices explicitly, the matrix element is
\[
\mathcal{M}
=
\left[
Q_\mu\overline{u}(p_4)(-ie\gamma^\mu)u(p_2)
\right]
\frac{-ig_{\mu\nu}}{q^2}
\left[
\overline{u}(p_3)(-ie\Gamma^\nu(Q^2))u(p_1)
\right].
\]

The contraction with the metric tensor identifies the two Lorentz indices. In particular,
\[
g_{\mu\nu}\Gamma^\nu(Q^2)=\Gamma_\mu(Q^2),
\]
where the index is lowered according to the metric. Thus, the propagator effectively connects the Lorentz index of the leptonic current to that of the hadronic current. The matrix element can therefore be written as
\[
\mathcal{M}
=
Q_\mu\frac{e^2}{q^2}
\,
\overline{u}(p_4)\gamma^\mu u(p_2)
\,
\overline{u}(p_3)\Gamma_\mu(Q^2)u(p_1).
\]

This expression is the starting point for calculating the cross section. However, the matrix element itself is not yet the observable quantity. The differential cross section is proportional to the squared modulus of the matrix element, summed over the unobserved final-state spins and averaged over the initial-state spins.

For unpolarized scattering, the spin-averaged squared matrix element is
defined as
\[
\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2}
=
\frac{1}{4}
\sum_{s_1,s_2,s_3,s_4}
|\mathcal{M}|^2,
\]
where the factor \(1/4\) averages over the two possible initial spin states of the muon and the two possible initial spin states of the proton.

Writing the spin labels explicitly, the matrix element is
\[
\mathcal{M}
=
Q_\mu\frac{e^2}{q^2}
\left[
\overline{u}(p_4,s_4)\gamma^\mu u(p_2,s_2)
\right]
\left[
\overline{u}(p_3,s_3)\Gamma_\mu(Q^2)u(p_1,s_1)
\right].
\]

Its complex conjugate is
\[
\mathcal{M}^*
=
Q_\mu\frac{e^2}{q^2}
\left[
\overline{u}(p_2,s_2)\gamma^\nu u(p_4,s_4)
\right]
\left[
\overline{u}(p_1,s_1)\overline{\Gamma}_\nu(Q^2)u(p_3,s_3)
\right],
\]
where
\[
\overline{\Gamma}_\nu
=
\gamma^0
\Gamma_\nu^\dagger
\gamma^0.
\]

Therefore,
\[
|\mathcal{M}|^2
=
\frac{e^4}{(q^2)^2}
\left[
\overline{u}(p_4)\gamma^\mu u(p_2)
\right]
\left[
\overline{u}(p_2)\gamma^\nu u(p_4)
\right]
\left[
\overline{u}(p_3)\Gamma_\mu u(p_1)
\right]
\left[
\overline{u}(p_1)\overline{\Gamma}_\nu u(p_3)
\right].
\]
To evaluate the spin sums, the completeness relation for Dirac spinors is
used:
\[
\sum_s u(p,s)\overline{u}(p,s)
=
\slashed{p}+m,
\]
where
\[
\slashed{p}
=
\gamma^\mu p_\mu.
\]

This relation allows the products of spinors to be converted into traces over products of gamma matrices. The spin-averaged squared matrix element can consequently be written as
\[
\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2}
=
\frac{e^4}{4(q^2)^2}
L^{\mu\nu}H_{\mu\nu},
\]
where the leptonic tensor is
\[
L^{\mu\nu}
=
\operatorname{Tr}
\left[
(\slashed{p}_4+m_\mu)
\gamma^\mu
(\slashed{p}_2+m_\mu)
\gamma^\nu
\right],
\]
and the hadronic tensor is
\[
H_{\mu\nu}
=
\operatorname{Tr}
\left[
(\slashed{p}_3+m_p)
\Gamma_\mu(Q^2)
(\slashed{p}_1+m_p)
\overline{\Gamma}_\nu(Q^2)
\right].
\]

The factorization into a leptonic and a hadronic tensor separates the two
physical contributions. The leptonic tensor describes the coupling of the
virtual photon to the muon, while the hadronic tensor contains the proton
electromagnetic structure through the form factors.

For the point-like muon, the leptonic tensor can be evaluated using the standard gamma-matrix trace identities. This gives

\begin{equation}
L^{\mu\nu}
=
4\left[
p_4^\mu p_2^\nu
+p_4^\nu p_2^\mu
-g^{\mu\nu}\left(p_2\cdot p_4-m_\mu^2\right)
\right].
\label{eq:massive_leptonic_tensor}
\end{equation}

The leptonic tensor obeys current conservation,
$q_\mu L^{\mu\nu}=q_\nu L^{\mu\nu}=0$.

To evaluate the hadronic tensor, it is convenient to introduce

\begin{equation}
\tau=\frac{Q^2}{4m_p^2}
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}

and the sum of the initial and final proton momenta,

\begin{equation}
P^\mu=p_1^\mu+p_3^\mu.
\end{equation}

The on-shell conditions imply $P\cdot q=0$ and
$P^2=4m_p^2+Q^2$. Using the Sachs form factors, the hadronic trace can then
be written in the compact form

\begin{equation}
H_{\mu\nu}
=
2\frac{G_E^2(Q^2)+\tau G_M^2(Q^2)}{1+\tau}
P_\mu P_\nu
-2G_M^2(Q^2)
\left(q_\mu q_\nu+Q^2g_{\mu\nu}\right).
\label{eq:hadronic_tensor_sachs}
\end{equation}

The terms containing $q_\mu q_\nu$ do not contribute to the contraction with
the conserved leptonic tensor. Defining

\begin{equation}
A(Q^2)
=
\frac{G_E^2(Q^2)+\tau G_M^2(Q^2)}{1+\tau},
\end{equation}

the contraction of \autoref{eq:massive_leptonic_tensor} with
\autoref{eq:hadronic_tensor_sachs} yields the exact spin-averaged squared
Born matrix element

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\overline{|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{Born}}|^2}
=\frac{e^4}{Q^4}\Bigl\{&
A(Q^2)
\left[
(s-u)^2-Q^2\left(4m_p^2+Q^2\right)
\right]
\\
&+2G_M^2(Q^2)Q^2\left(Q^2-2m_\mu^2\right)
\Bigr\}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:born_matrix_element_massive}
\end{equation}

Inserting \autoref{eq:born_matrix_element_massive} into \autoref{eq:diff_cross_section} gives the exact laboratory-frame Born cross section,

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\frac{d\sigma_{\mathrm{Born}}}{d\Omega}
={}&
\frac{1}{64\pi^2m_p p_\mu}
\frac{{p'_\mu}^{\,2}}
{p'_\mu(m_p+E_\mu)-p_\mu E'_\mu\cos\theta_\mu}
\frac{e^4}{Q^4}
\\
&\times
\left\{
A(Q^2)
\left[
(s-u)^2-Q^2\left(4m_p^2+Q^2\right)
\right]
+2G_M^2(Q^2)Q^2\left(Q^2-2m_\mu^2\right)
\right\}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:born_cross_section}
\end{equation}






In the ultrarelativisitic limit, the Leptonic tensor simplifies to
\[
L^{\mu\nu}
=
4
\left(
p_4^\mu p_2^\nu
+
p_4^\nu p_2^\mu
-
g^{\mu\nu}p_4\cdot p_2
\right).
\]

and the hadronic tensor reduces to !!!!!check this!!!!!!
\[
H_{\mu\nu}
=       
2\frac{G_E^2(Q^2)+\tau G_M^2(Q^2)}{1+\tau} 
P_\mu P_\nu . 
\]

Evaluating the leptonic and hadronic tensors and performing the contraction \(L^{\mu\nu}H_{\mu\nu}\) then leads to the Rosenbluth form of the elastic Born cross section.

\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\sigma_{\mathrm{Born}}}{d\Omega}
    =
    \frac{d\sigma_{\mathrm{Mott}}}{d\Omega}
    \frac{\tau}{\epsilon(1+\tau)}
    \left(
        G_M^2(Q^2)
        +
        \frac{\epsilon}{\tau}G_E^2(Q^2)
    \right),
    \label{eq:born_cross_section}
\end{equation}

where $\frac{d\sigma_{\mathrm{Mott}}}{d\Omega}$ is the Mott cross section which describes the elastic scattering of a charged lepton from a structureless charged target. In the ultrarelativistic limit, it is given by

\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\sigma_{\mathrm{Mott}}}{d\Omega}
    =
    \frac{\alpha^2 \cos^2(\theta/2)}
    {4E^2\sin^4(\theta/2)}.
    \label{eq:mott_cross_section}
\end{equation}

, where the the so-called virtual photon polarization parameter \(\epsilon\) is given by
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon
    =
    \left(
        1+2(1+\tau)\tan^2\frac{\theta}{2}
    \right)^{-1}.
    \label{eq:epsilon}
\end{equation}

In the low-$Q^2$ region relevant for proton radius measurements, the Sachs form factors can be approximated by the empirical dipole parametrization

\begin{equation}
    G_D(Q^2)
    =
    \left(
        1+\frac{Q^2}{\Lambda^2}
    \right)^{-2},
    \label{eq:dipole_form_factor}
\end{equation}

where the dipole mass parameter is

\begin{equation}
    \Lambda^2 = 0.71\,\mathrm{GeV}^2.
\end{equation}

The electric and magnetic form factors are then expressed as

\begin{equation}
    G_E(Q^2)
    =
    G_D(Q^2)
    =
    \left(
        1+\frac{Q^2}{0.71\,\mathrm{GeV}^2}
    \right)^{-2},\footnote{For a point-like proton, the form factor would be \(G_E(Q^2)=1\) for all \(Q^2\).}
    \label{eq:GE_dipole}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    G_M(Q^2)
    =
    \mu_p G_D(Q^2)
    =
    \mu_p
    \left(
        1+\frac{Q^2}{0.71\,\mathrm{GeV}^2}
    \right)^{-2},
    \label{eq:GM_dipole}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_p = 2.792847$ is the dimensionless proton magnetic moment in
units of the nuclear magneton $\mu_N$. It defines the normalization of the
magnetic form factor, such that $G_M(0)=\mu_p$.


\section{Radiative Corrections}
This thesis considers radiative corrections through next-to-leading order
(NLO) in QED. The Born amplitude is of order $e^2$ and gives a cross section
of order $\alpha^2$. The NLO contribution to the cross section is of order
$\alpha^3$ and receives contributions from both virtual one-loop amplitudes
and real-photon emission. The corresponding Feynman diagrams are shown in
\autoref{fig:processes}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.\textwidth]{figures/Processes_Mehran.pdf}
    \caption{Leading-order and next-to-leading-order contributions to elastic muon--proton scattering. Figure provided by M. Depour.}
    \label{fig:processes}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Virtual corrections.}
Virtual corrections arise from one-loop diagrams and include self-energy,
vertex, vacuum-polarization, and, where included in the chosen correction
class, two-photon-exchange contributions. They modify the Born amplitude
through the interference term between the one-loop and Born amplitudes.
Ultraviolet divergences are removed by renormalization, whereas infrared
divergences cancel only after the corresponding real-emission contribution
is included.

\paragraph{Real photon emission.}
The real contribution is the bremsstrahlung process
$p\mu\rightarrow p\mu\gamma$, in which an additional photon is emitted from
a charged external particle. Its phase space must be combined with the
virtual contribution using the same definition of the measured observable.
The resulting sum is infrared finite. Experimentally, its numerical value
also depends on acceptance requirements and photon-energy cuts.
